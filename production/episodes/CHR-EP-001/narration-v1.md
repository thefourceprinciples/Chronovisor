# CHR-EP-001 — Mūmiyāʾ: The Substance Before the Corpse

## Production narration draft

In eighteenth-century Europe, you could walk into an apothecary and purchase something called *mumia*.

It was medicine.

And sometimes, chemical evidence suggests, it really did contain material from an ancient Egyptian mummy.

For centuries, European physicians prescribed mummy-derived substances for bleeding, wounds, bruising, and other ailments.

It is tempting to explain this with one bizarre sentence: Europeans consumed Egyptian mummies because somebody mistranslated a word.

But that is not what happened.

Long before European apothecaries sold powdered human remains, physicians of the ancient Mediterranean were already familiar with bitumen and asphalt.

Before *mummy* meant a preserved corpse, there was *mūmiyāʾ* — a rare, dark substance associated especially with the rocks of Persia.

Across medieval Arabic pharmacology, its most distinguished indication was the treatment of fractures.

A substance famous for rejoining what had been broken.

Persian geographical traditions then surround the substance with stories of guarded sources, restricted access, annual collection, and royal custody.

But we should be careful. The evidence does not establish one singular magical cave. Different writers place the source in different locations, suggesting that several deposits may have been gathered into a prestige geography.

A remarkable Byzantine account carries the story further. Mūmiyāʾ appears as a diplomatic gift, a priceless medicine associated with a guarded rock that released droplets like tears. The text even describes an animal test: dose an injured creature, bandage it, wait several days, and observe the result.

That is not a clinical trial. But it is a structured premodern validation ritual: injury, dose, interval, observation.

And then the dead enter the story.

Arabic medical authors knew a corpse-associated variety of mūmiyāʾ. The category was no longer confined to one material.

Mineral pitch. Asphalt. Embalming material. Material from graves.

The word was beginning to travel across substances that looked, felt, or were believed to be related.

Then Egypt supplied a dangerous partial truth.

Modern chemical research shows that petroleum bitumen was not present in every Egyptian mummy. But it did occur frequently in many later mummies, especially in Ptolemaic and Roman-period examples.

So the European association between mummies and bitumen was neither universally true nor imaginary.

It was a partial material truth attached to an increasingly unstable category.

The original mineral was rare. Demand was persistent. Authentication was weak.

Eventually the distinction between medicine, embalming material, and corpse became commercially dangerous.

The merchant did not always need the original mineral.

Eventually, the merchant did not even need an ancient Egyptian mummy.

All that was necessary was something capable of passing as *mumia*.

Critics such as Ambroise Paré attacked the practice, but the trade had already demonstrated something larger: once a prestigious medicinal category becomes materially elastic, commerce can preserve the name after the substance beneath it has changed.

And then there is the physical evidence.

An eighteenth-century pharmaceutical vessel labeled MUMIA has actually been chemically examined. The material inside contained textile fibers, waxes, resins, fats, and other markers consistent with authentic Egyptian mummy material, alongside bituminous material.

At least some historical pharmaceutical stock was therefore exactly what its label claimed.

The category had completed another transformation.

Material once sold as medicine for damaged flesh could itself become flesh used as medicine — and eventually a pigment used to paint flesh.

So why did Europeans consume mummies?

Not because one translator made one mistake.

The evidence points toward something much slower.

A rare mineral medicine became famous.

Its identity was already unstable across languages.

Similar dark substances became associated with it.

Some Egyptian embalming traditions genuinely used petroleum bitumen.

Material taken from embalmed bodies entered the same pharmaceutical category.

The body inherited the category.

Demand exceeded supply.

And commerce erased whatever distinctions remained.

What happened was not a single mistranslation.

It was a centuries-long category collapse.

Mūmiyāʾ began as something hidden in stone.

A waxlike mineral became a royal drug.

The royal drug became an embalming substance.

The embalming substance became a body.

The body became merchandise.

The merchandise became medicine — and paint.

And the word survived after nearly every material distinction beneath it had disappeared.

History leaves fragments.

We reconstruct what happened.
